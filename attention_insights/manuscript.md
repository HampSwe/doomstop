## 1. Opening story

Three years ago, the night before my biggest exam, I decided to skip sleep entirely.
I had coffee, I had snacks, and I had a plan to read every chapter one more time.
At four in the morning I looked at a page I had read ten times and realized I could not
remember a single word of it. The next day I walked into that exam and my mind was
completely blank. That night taught me something I want to share with you today:
sleep is not time off for your brain. It is when your brain does some of its most
important work.

## 2. Definitions and numbers

Let me start with some definitions. Sleep is usually divided into two main types:
non rapid eye movement sleep, which has three stages, and rapid eye movement sleep.
A full cycle lasts roughly ninety minutes, and a typical night contains four to six
cycles. Stage one makes up about five percent of the night, stage two about forty
five to fifty five percent, stage three about fifteen to twenty five percent, and
rapid eye movement sleep about twenty to twenty five percent. These proportions shift
with age, and they also vary from night to night within the same person.

## 3. Audience question

Now a quick question for all of you. Raise your hand if you have ever woken up and
suddenly known the answer to a problem that seemed impossible the evening before.
Look around the room. That is a lot of hands! This is not luck. While you sleep, your
brain replays what you learned during the day and connects it to things you already
know. Some of the most famous scientific ideas, like the structure of the periodic
table, were reportedly finished in a dream.

## 4. Study methodology

To understand how researchers measure this, let me describe the methodology of a
typical study. Participants are recruited and screened for sleep disorders. They spend
an adaptation night in the laboratory so the unfamiliar environment does not affect
the results. Electrodes are attached to the scalp to record an electroencephalogram,
and additional sensors measure eye movements and muscle tone. Participants learn a list
of word pairs in the evening, they are tested once before sleep, and then they are
tested again in the morning. The control group stays awake for the same interval.
The data is scored in thirty second epochs according to standardized criteria.

## 5. Surprising result

And here is the surprising part. In many of these experiments, the people who slept
did not just forget less. Some of them actually got better than they were the night
before, without any extra practice. Imagine learning a piano piece, going to bed, and
waking up able to play it faster and with fewer mistakes. Your brain literally
practiced while you were unconscious. Even a short nap of twenty minutes can give you
a measurable boost.
